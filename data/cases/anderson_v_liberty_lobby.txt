Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986)
United States Supreme Court
Topic: Summary judgment — the "genuine issue of material fact" standard

Excerpt:

The Court clarified what a "genuine" issue of "material" fact means for
purposes of Rule 56. A fact is "material" if it might affect the outcome
of the suit under the governing substantive law; factual disputes that are
irrelevant or unnecessary will not defeat summary judgment.

An issue is "genuine" if the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could
return a verdict for the nonmoving party. If the evidence is merely
colorable, or is not significantly probative, summary judgment may be
granted.

Critically, the judge must view the evidence through the prism of the
substantive evidentiary burden that would apply at trial. In a case
requiring proof by clear and convincing evidence, the court on summary
judgment must ask whether a reasonable factfinder could find that the
plaintiff had shown the claim by clear and convincing evidence. The
inquiry is whether the evidence presents a sufficient disagreement to
require submission to a jury or whether it is so one-sided that one party
must prevail as a matter of law. The court does not weigh the evidence or
make credibility determinations; those are jury functions.
